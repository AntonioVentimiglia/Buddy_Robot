# Engineering Log — August 2026

## 2026-08-01 — The UART bug was real, narrower than filed, and unreproducible
**Tags:** firmware, test, failure
- **Context:** on 31 July I filed a bench TODO claiming that *any* UART error
  would silently kill the drive MCU's receive path while telemetry kept
  streaming. Today's job was to fix it. The first step was to reproduce it.
- **The mechanism, drawn:** `assets/figures/uart_rx_recovery.svg` (generated by
  `tools/figures/plot_uart_rx_recovery.py`). RX is a **self-re-arming chain** —
  `HAL_UART_Receive_IT` arms exactly one byte and `HAL_UART_RxCpltCallback` arms
  the next. There is no free-running receiver. Break the chain once and it never
  restarts. TX is a separate path, which is what makes the failure deceptive:
  the MCU keeps transmitting at 100 Hz and looks healthy while ignoring
  every command, so the Jetson gets blamed.
- **The test failed to reproduce it — and that was the useful part.** Injecting
  a real bus error (garbage written at 9600 into a 921600 receiver, plus a break
  condition) left PING→PONG at 5/5. The link recovered on its own.
- **Reading the HAL source settled it.** `stm32g4xx_hal_uart.c` splits errors
  two ways, in an explicit comment:
  - **FE / NE / PE — non-blocking.** Reception continues; the callback is
    informational and nothing is required of us.
  - **ORE / RTO — blocking.** `UART_EndRxTransfer()` disables the RX interrupts,
    *then* the weak, empty `HAL_UART_ErrorCallback` is called. Nothing re-arms.

  So the bug is real, but only for **overrun** — not "any UART error" as filed.
  My injection had been exercising the branch that heals itself.
- **Then ORE refused to happen either.** A sustained flood at line rate —
  4 kB, 32 kB, then 128 kB back-to-back — never overran the ISR. At 921600 a byte
  arrives every ~10.8 µs, which is ~1800 cycles at 170 MHz; a one-byte ISR
  finishes with room to spare. The dangerous branch is **not reachable from the
  host** on this setup.
- **Fixed anyway, and labelled honestly.** `HAL_UART_ErrorCallback` now clears
  the overrun flag and re-arms reception — six lines, +48 bytes of flash. It is
  **insurance, not a repair**: the realistic ORE sources are on-board (a long
  higher-priority ISR, a critical section, or motor EMI on the cable once the
  drivers are live), and none of those exist yet. The verification script's
  docstring states plainly which branch it covers, so a future reader cannot
  mistake a pass for proof of the overrun path.
- **The figure got corrected too.** My first version showed ORE/FE/NE all killing
  RX, which is wrong and was heading for the website. It now shows the branch
  that self-recovers alongside the one that doesn't — which is the actually
  interesting content, and the thing I would have wanted to know.
- **Lesson.** Two days ago the pattern was *the instrument is wrong more often
  than the system*. Today's is adjacent but distinct: **a bug report written
  from inspection is a hypothesis, not a finding.** I filed "any UART error kills
  RX" from reading our own code and noticing an absence. Reading *ST's* code
  narrowed it to one error class; trying to trigger that class showed it is
  currently unreachable. The fix survived all three stages, but the claim
  attached to it shrank at each one — and the shrunken claim is the one worth
  recording.
- Regression: MCU link 13/13, bridge-vs-real-MCU 13/13, full host suite green.

## 2026-08-01 — Sourcing the parts broke the power architecture, and fixed it
**Tags:** decision, calc, purchase, failure
- **The task was a shopping list. The output was an ADR amendment.** Compiling
  sourced options for every unbought part (`docs/financials/PARTS_OPTIONS_v0_1.md`,
  32 links) surfaced two problems that made the specified architecture
  unbuildable — both invisible while the spec stayed abstract.
- **Failure 1 — the Jetson's 12 V rail could not exist.** The shopping list
  called for a *12 V buck*. A step-down needs input above output, and a 3S bus is
  12.6 V only at full charge, spending most of its discharge below 12 V and
  bottoming at 9.0 V. The specified part would have dropped out almost
  immediately. The named example, `D24V50F12`, **does not appear to exist** —
  Pololu's fixed 12 V step-downs are 500 mA, and the 5 A `D24V50F5` is a 5 V part.
  A plausible-sounding part number had been carried in the plan for three weeks.
- **Failure 2 — the ≥50 A BMS was not purchasable in 3S.** Stock "12 V" Li-ion
  packs ship 10–30 A boards against a 46 A designed peak. That is a pack which
  trips on the first carpet pivot — precisely the failure the protection chain
  exists to prevent, and precisely what the shopping list's own warning
  predicted. Remaining routes were "fit your own BMS" or "build from cells":
  neither is a purchase.
- **Both had one answer: 4S LiFePO4.** 10.0–14.6 V sits inside the Jetson dev
  kit's 9–20 V window with margin at both ends, so the computer feeds **straight
  off the bus and the converter is deleted rather than re-specified**; and a
  100 A BMS is the default in that category rather than something to hunt for.
- **Recomputed rather than asserted** (`buddy_calcs/power.py`): system current
  4.95 → **4.30 A**, designed peak 37.0 → **36.3 A**, peak incl. arms 47.0 →
  **46.3 A**, capacity target ≥14 → **≥12.1 Ah** (energy unchanged at 155 Wh —
  the volts went up), torque at the 8 A limit **unchanged at 3.23 N·m** because
  torque per amp is voltage-independent. BMS ≥50 A and the 60 A fuse both stand.
  `validate()` passes.
- **Costs, recorded rather than buried:** ~+1.3 kg of mass, larger than the
  +0.6–0.8 kg the 07-14 amendment absorbed; and the 12 V motors run above nominal
  (~238 RPM at 12.8 V, ~271 at 14.6 V, stall current 9.2 → ~11.2 A at full
  charge). The speed is spare headroom — required RPM comes from the speed
  requirements, never the motor's no-load figure — and the stall current moves
  only the *fault* scenario. The mass is the real one, and **chassis CAD is where
  it gets settled, not assumed.**
- **Renamed** `ADR-0005-power-architecture-3s-liion.md` →
  `...-battery-bus.md`. A filename asserting a chemistry the ADR no longer
  chooses is the same class of defect as the stale Mermaid diagram deleted on
  07-28: wrong, and quietly authoritative.
- **Lesson.** A specification is not a design until someone tries to buy it. Both
  failures were latent in a document that had been reviewed, amended, and cited
  for three weeks; neither was findable by reading it more carefully. The
  forcing function was pricing real parts against real vendors, and it is
  cheaper to do that before the money moves than after.
